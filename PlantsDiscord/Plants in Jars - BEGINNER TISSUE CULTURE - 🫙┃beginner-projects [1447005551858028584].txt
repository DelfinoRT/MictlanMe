[1/16/2026 4:21 AM] hieuhuule
I'm germinating seeds in-vitro! This was actually a bit of work since I used a bleach method instead of hydrogen peroxide, but I can try that next time.

I did this in 3 big steps:
1. Making the media
2. Sterilizing the seeds
3. Planting the seeds in the media

I had to make media without hormones specifically for this because the other media I made was for multiplication. This will also be the recipe for when im storing explants long term. They'll still grow naturally, but without the added plant growth regulators (PGRs), it will be a lot slower.  

The media recipe I used was scaled down to fill four 5.5 oz polypropylene deli containers. This is the recipe I used and its called MS0, because its just MS without any hormones:

Distilled water: 167 mL
MS salts: 0.73 g
Sucrose: 5.0 g
Agar: 1.33 g
PPM: 0.17 mL (≈ 170 µL)
pH: 5.8 

While the media was cooling after autoclaving in a pressure cooker, I also made two jars of sterile distilled water to work with. These were important for the cleaning the seeds.

To clean the seeds, I rinsed the seeds in 5 steps:
1. 1 minute in 70% Isopropyl Alcohol.
2. 10 minutes in a 1:9 parts sterile water and bleach with 1 drop of dish soap.
3. 1 minute rinse with sterile water.
4. 1 minute rinse with sterile water.
5. 1 minute rinse with sterile water.

By the 3rd rinse with the water, it'll be ready to plant into the media. After planting, I sealed it up and put it on my tissue culture shelf under 16 hours of light and 8 hours of darkness.

With any luck, I should have clean germination of the seeds.

[7/27/2026 9:01 PM] muddledmindedmagus
Huh, this is an interesting concept, though. Coming at it from a pure genetics perspective, you'd also have to consider how long they are been hybridized for. If its a sterile hybrid offspring between two closely related species/subspecies/cultivars thats been propagated for a long while, theres the chance that even if you were to duplicate and separate out the chromosomes, the offspring would be inviable due to mutations that have arisen since hybridization. 

Ie, after hybridization, you now have 2 alleles that no longer recombine/purge deleterious mutations. Allele 1 from parent A gets a deleterious mutation, so it no longer works. Allele 2 from parent B is still functioning, so the lineage persists. But if you were to duplicate Allele 1  from parent A and separate it, the offspring wouldnt live. 

Similarly, even within heavily inbred lines (which are nearly the same at every location), you can still get heterozygous sites that persist because homozygosity would be lethal.

[7/27/2026 10:13 PM] muddledmindedmagus
Unfortunately, I feel like it just got less likely. The longer you propagate a line asexually, the more different it becomes from what it originally was. 1000-1500 years in an organism that doesnt need vernalization would mean easily 1000+ generations. Even after a few generations, weird chromosomal stuff can happen.

The study that comes to mind is where they cloned a mouse for like 50 generations, and sequenced it along the way. It lost entire sections of chromosomes because they werent being maintained (including even the X chromosome at one point). 

Also, its pretty important to keep in mind that even "sterile" organisms can sometimes reproduce. Through random chance, they might actually be able to cross, though very very unlikely. Over a thousand years, its possible someone managed to cross it with something else (although unlikely)

Also, theres other stuff that can happen that can change the genome. Imagine if it got infected with a virus that inserts itself into the genome (or some other jumping genes). It can cause drastic differences in the organism's phenotype through inserting itself into certain genes (the thing that comes to mind is blue coloring in chicken eggs via a retrovirus!)

[7/28/2026 8:40 PM] muddledmindedmagus
Independent assortment is going to be rough with so many chromosomes, so the "sets" very likely wont separate together. You went from 2^8 gamete types (=256), to 3*8 gamete types (=6561)

Doesn't a complete knockdown of DMC1 cause infertility? Maybe I'm only thinking of animals, I'm not too sure about plants. 

Polyploidy also does weird things to the organisms, so the "traits" likely wouldnt be what you expect (ie, modern strawberries are octoploid from diploid/tetraploid ancestors, and look completely different). 

Another thing to look into - dosage issues (expanding on the last sentence). Where you had two copies of a gene before, now you have 6. This can cause a lot of issues when dosage matters (general issue with almost all polyploid individuals). You started with a F1 C1 individual, and ended with F F C1 C1 C2 C2 (or some variation thereof). the F set now has double the dosage, while the C set has anywhere from 0 to 4x, and anything shared between them could have as high as 6x. Adding to that it wouldn't be standard across the genome (Say, chromosome 1 from C1 is in the cross (F F C1 C1 C1 C1, 2x of F, 4x of C1, 0x of C2), but chromosome 2 differes (F F C1 C2 C2 C2. 2x of F, 1x of C1, 3x of C2). IIRC, polyploidy is much more successful when there arent dosage differences (although, plants do this much better than animals). 

"i just hope I can find the unique centromeric satellite repeats" 
This would probably be an interesting first step. Are the species sequenced with a reference genome assembled? How good is the reference genome (do they have high confidence that it's assembled properly?) Speaking from experience, repetitive regions and centromeric regions are a pain in the butt to assemble, as neither make sequencing/assembly easy. You also said that your cultivar may be a different cross than others, so youd also want to make sure that its your cross that was sequenced.
